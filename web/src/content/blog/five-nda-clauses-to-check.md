Non-disclosure agreements are among the most frequently signed documents in any business. Because they are so familiar, they are also among the most frequently under-reviewed. A single broad definition or an unexpected restrictive covenant can create obligations that far outlast the deal they were meant to support.

Below are five clauses we recommend checking on every NDA, whether it arrives on your own paper or the counterparty's.

## 1. The definition of Confidential Information

The definition sets the scope of everything that follows. Watch for language that captures *all* information "of any nature, whether or not marked as confidential". Very broad definitions can make compliance impractical and disputes more likely.

**What to look for:**

- Whether information must be marked or identified as confidential
- Whether oral disclosures are covered, and if they must be confirmed in writing
- Whether the definition is limited to information disclosed for the stated purpose

## 2. Exclusions and carve-outs

Standard exclusions protect the recipient from being bound in relation to information it already had or could legitimately obtain elsewhere. Their absence is a meaningful deviation.

Typical carve-outs cover information that is or becomes public through no fault of the recipient, was already known to the recipient, is independently developed, or is received from a third party without restriction.

## 3. Term and survival

Check both how long the agreement lasts and how long confidentiality obligations survive after it ends. Perpetual obligations may be appropriate for trade secrets, but they are often unnecessary for ordinary commercial information.

## 4. Restrictive covenants hiding in plain sight

Non-solicitation and, occasionally, non-compete provisions sometimes appear in NDAs. They are easy to miss and can be significantly more onerous than the confidentiality obligations themselves.

> A 36-month non-solicitation clause covering *all* employees is a very different commitment from one limited to individuals directly involved in the engagement.

## 5. Remedies, indemnities and governing law

Look for indemnities, liquidated damages or provisions that make the recipient liable for "all losses" arising from any breach. Confirm the governing law and forum are acceptable — for example, whether disputes are resolved onshore in the UAE, in the DIFC or ADGM courts, or by arbitration.

## Building consistency

The most effective way to review NDAs quickly is to agree a standard position on each of these points in advance, along with acceptable fallbacks. That playbook allows routine agreements to move fast, while genuine deviations are escalated to the right person.
